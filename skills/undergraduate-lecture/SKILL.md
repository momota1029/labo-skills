# Write for an undergraduate mathematics reader

Before substantive work, follow [eligibility and handoff](../research-router/references/editing-capability.md).

Use this skill for an undergraduate-targeted lecture note or a manuscript
target named `*_lec.tex`. It specializes the explanation; it does not create a
second production workflow. For a research lecture manuscript, also follow
[paper-writing](../paper-writing/SKILL.md) for the target, authorial intent and
normal workspace workflow. For substantial mathematical explanation, apply
[mathematical-exposition](../mathematical-exposition/SKILL.md) as well.

Apply [mathematical substance and proof detail](../mathematical-exposition/references/mathematical-substance.md)
when writing or revising the mathematical content. The goal is a correct lecture
whose proofs and calculations the intended reader can follow, including the
intermediate steps, not only an understandable account of the proof strategy.

Use the reader prerequisites established by the user and the material. For a
research-paper lecture with no other audience specified, calculus, linear algebra,
basic set/function notation and elementary proof reading may be assumed. For a
foundational course or textbook, prepare the needed background in the text rather
than assuming those entire subjects. Advanced subject matter must not silently
raise the proof-reading threshold: keep remaining gaps fillable from introduced
results and early-undergraduate mathematical operations.

## Plan only the needed background

For a substantial rewrite, locate the concepts that carry mathematical weight.
For each one, identify its first important use, what the reader already knows,
and the smallest bridge that makes the use understandable. This can be a
working model, a small example, an operational definition, a theorem's input
and output, or a link to an earlier idea. Introduce background where the proof
needs it. A bounded paper explanation need not expand into a textbook; a course
or textbook request does require the background and intermediate proofs needed
for its declared reader.

## Develop the proof and calculations

Design proof stages from the first draft: give each substantial stage a concrete
purpose, carry out its argument, and connect its conclusion to the next stage.
Use [mathematical-navigation](../mathematical-navigation/SKILL.md) for heading/number
colors, cross-references and indexes while drafting and checking the lecture.
Step headings alone do not fill proof gaps.

State what a result says in usable terms and decode the hypotheses that matter.
Before a specialist object carries an argument, explain its role and the
structure needed here. A background theorem may remain a black box when its
input, output and applicability are explicit and the lecture's scope permits
using it as a stated tool. Keep the decisive idea, main obstruction and closing
estimate visible. Also carry out the intermediate deductions and calculations;
a description of their purpose or a citation does not discharge them.

Use a concrete or finite-dimensional example when it clarifies the next
abstraction. Check the example's hypotheses and calculations and label a special
case as such. Preserve the source's useful mathematical progression while adding
what this reader needs. In lecture creation, repair faulty claims, incomplete
proofs and unusable formulations, verify the corrected mathematics and its later
applications, and record the source correspondence. Do not retain an error for
fidelity or stop at a warning when the lecture requires a repair. Faithful
translation and report-only review retain their separate task boundaries.

## Read the note as its audience

Check the first use of each important object and whether the local explanation
lets the reader follow the next inference. At natural stopping points, check
whether the reader can say what the result means, why the argument needs its
tools, or where the hard step lies. Repair a real barrier at its first use and
batch related changes. A model-based reading can reveal a barrier, but it does
not establish that real students understand the note; preserve any required
human review.
