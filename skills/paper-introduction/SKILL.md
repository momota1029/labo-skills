# Discover the question; earn the introduction

Follow the [required consultation](../research-router/references/consultation.md)
once for the request; reuse a completed consultation when passing between skills.

Keep ownership clear:

- Use [paper-writing](../paper-writing/SKILL.md) for manuscript editing,
  authorship, workspace rules/corpus, evidence status and production. Read it
  before drafting or changing manuscript prose; a narrative assessment alone
  does not authorize edits. Preserve the author's voice and intended emphasis.
- Use [reading-reference](../reading-reference/SKILL.md) for source investigation
  and its [positioning protocol](../reading-reference/references/research-positioning.md)
  for substantive framing. Reuse its current memo and refresh affected evidence;
  do not create a competing survey, novelty gate or memo schema here. A bounded
  assessment reports positioning gaps without silently modifying records.
- Apply [mathematical-exposition](../mathematical-exposition/SKILL.md) for reader
  prerequisites, terminology and inferential clarity. Let `paper-writing` route
  requested rendered manuscripts to `labo-build`.

## 1. Discover what the paper is really about

Start from the requested audience and existing author intent. Read the relevant
draft, research plan, actual theorem/proof records or empirical evidence, and
the positioning memo's supporting comparisons. Follow only dependencies needed
to understand the central contribution. Do not manufacture a story from a title,
abstract, theorem list or plan while leaving decisive evidence unread.

Make a compact working account, not a mandatory new file:

- **Question or phenomenon:** What object, behavior or relationship is to be
  understood? What would an answer explain or make possible? Distinguish this
  scientific question from the task of proving a particular displayed theorem.
- **Author's interesting point:** What surprise, connection, obstruction,
  example or change of viewpoint is the author trying to expose? Check it
  against the mathematics; do not replace it with the easiest novelty slogan.
- **Supported answer:** Which exact result answers which part of the question,
  under what hypotheses, and with what proof or experimental status? Inspect
  enough of the argument to identify the decisive move, not just its name.
- **Reason to care:** What becomes understandable, accessible or usable through
  this answer? Identify a concrete consequence or explanatory gain and its
  evidence, not a generic claim that the field is important.

Treat tensions between the plan, author's account, proof and draft as discoveries
to resolve. Prefer the supported mathematical boundary; flag consequential
changes of emphasis or claim for the author. If essential material is missing,
identify the exact missing statement or argument and offer a bounded provisional
outline. Ask only questions whose answers would change the narrative choice.

## 2. Establish the relationship, then the value

Use inspected primary evidence on both sides of each material comparison.
Describe what earlier work successfully enables before explaining what changes.
Distinguish inherited definitions, tools and results from the present argument.
Name the precise relationship: application, special case, reformulation,
extension, refinement, alternative proof, incomparable result or unresolved
comparison. Test an extension by recovering the predecessor and identifying
the actual additional case or conclusion; different vocabulary is not enough.

Find the credible value of that relationship. A wider theorem is one possible
value, not the sole criterion. A reformulation may expose a mechanism, unify
cases or permit a useful calculation; an alternative proof may make a dependency
or obstruction visible. State such gains only when the paper demonstrates
them. Separate an established consequence from a proposed application or hope.
Do not invent an unsolved gap to justify work with a different legitimate aim.

Keep unresolved comparisons visible. Do not turn no search hits into priority,
or “not covered by this source” into “not previously known.” Avoid unsupported
“first,” “complete,” “general,” “optimal,” or superiority claims. A limited
contribution can be introduced confidently by saying exactly what it does.

## 3. Choose a route for this reader

Arrange information by what the reader needs to understand the next claim,
not by the chronology of the research or a compulsory paragraph template.
Choose the opening that makes the real question tangible with the least
unsupported background: a concrete phenomenon/example, a precise question,
an inherited capability, or an already meaningful result. Supply context only
as it becomes necessary. Do not delay the paper's actual subject behind a broad
importance speech or bait whose promised consequence the paper never earns.

Make a paragraph-level outline with each paragraph's job and its transition.
Test that a reader can recover the question, prior capability, present change,
main answer, reason it matters and limits. These are dependencies to satisfy,
not six mandatory paragraphs or a fixed sequence. For example:

- For a conceptual puzzle, begin with an illuminating instance, formulate the
  question it raises, and use prior work to make the answer's significance clear.
- For a specialist extension, begin near the shared result, explain the exact
  changed boundary, and reveal the new mechanism and theorem promptly.
- For a reinterpretation, establish the existing capability, show what the new
  viewpoint reveals, and explain the demonstrated payoff without pretending the
  inherited theorem was unavailable.

Select one coherent route; do not concatenate all patterns. Put just enough
definition before an informal result to make it meaningful. Decide whether a
formal theorem belongs here or later based on notation cost and audience; do
not erase consequential hypotheses to make a statement sound simpler. Include
proof ideas when they explain why the question is answerable or what the paper
adds, rather than as a list of technical tools. Add a section roadmap only when
it helps the reader navigate a non-obvious structure or a venue requires it.

## 4. Draft and audit against the evidence

Write the requested outline, passage or introduction, not merely advice about
writing one. Keep research claims, author motivations and explanatory framing
distinguishable. Credit inherited work at the relevant statement with precise
citations. Preserve applicable domains, quantifiers, parameter ranges and
limitations, including finite/infinite, local/global, generic/all, asymptotic/
finite-sample and existence/constructive distinctions. Distinguish proved,
conjectured, observed and formally checked results at their actual scope.

Audit in both directions:

1. Trace each substantive introduction claim to an inspected result, argument
   or primary comparison. Check that the advertised theorem and proof idea agree
   with the body; nearby Lean declarations or build success do not expand it.
2. Check that the central interesting point and supported contribution actually
   appear, with enough context for the intended reader to say why they matter.
3. Read in order for missing definitions, unexplained changes of viewpoint,
   duplicated background and promises not fulfilled by the paper. Keep clear
   passages rather than rewriting merely to match a pattern.

Return the requested prose first. Separately give the consequential narrative
choices, evidence boundaries and remaining author decisions as needed. Do not
insert editorial uncertainty notes into polished prose as if they were results,
or hide unresolved support behind confident language. Report actual checks,
not proof validation, exhaustive novelty verification or submission readiness
that this narrative workflow cannot establish.
